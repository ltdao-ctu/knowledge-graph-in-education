A Case-Driven and Knowledge Graph-Supported
Approach to Pathology Education: Effects on
Knowledge Retention and Clinical Reasoning

Hui Wang, Hua Ni, Xia Ting, Jiang-Hua Wu, Xin-Xia Tian

Department of Pathology, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Peking University Third Hospital, Peking University Health Science Center, Beijing,
People’s Republic of China

Correspondence: Xin-Xia Tian, Department of Pathology, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Peking University Third Hospital, Peking University Health
Science Center, 38 Xueyuan Road, Haidian District, Beijing, 100191, People’s Republic of China, Email tianxinxia@bjmu.edu.cn

Objective:  Given  the  issues  of  knowledge  fragmentation  and  insufficient  clinical  reasoning  associated  with  traditional  linear
pathology  teaching,  this  study  developed  and  evaluated  a  “case-driven,  knowledge-graph-supported”  curriculum  restructuring
model.  The  model  was  designed  to  shift  the  instructional  approach  from  a  discipline-centric  logic  toward  a  clinical  diagnostic
reasoning framework.
Methods:  Fifty-four  five-year  clinical  medicine  undergraduates  at  Peking  University  Health  Science  Center  participated.  Using
clinical cases as the organizing framework, the “Urinary System Pathology” course was restructured into a knowledge graph-supported
progressive learning framework. A mixed-methods evaluation design was adopted to comprehensively assess teaching effectiveness,
including  three  in-session  formative  tests,  a  14-day  delayed  knowledge  retention  assessment,  and  a  structured  student  learning
experience questionnaire.
Results: Students  showed  significant  improvements  across  the  three  immediate  in-session  formative  tests,  with  median  percentage
scores of 76.6%, 87.8%, and 85.9%, respectively (P < 0.001). In the day-14 retention test, the median percentage score under open-
book conditions was higher than the average of the three in-session tests (91.8% vs 83.5%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the closed-book
median  percentage  score  was  also  higher  than  that  of  the  three  in-session  tests  (89.0%  vs  83.5%,  P  =  0.016),  with  no  significant
difference  found  between  open-  and  closed-book  conditions  (P  =  0.69).  Students’ self-rated  competency  in  renal  disease  diagnosis
showed  a  marked  increase,  with  the  average  score  rising  from  2.0  to  4.4  (P  <  0.001).  However,  alongside  these  positive  learning
outcomes, the course also presented notable challenges for a subset of learners. 14.8% of participants reported challenges related to
course pace and excessive cognitive load during the learning process.
Conclusion:  The  proposed  model  was  associated  with  improved  student  performance  in  our  cohort.  These  preliminary  findings
suggest potential benefits for addressing knowledge fragmentation and inert knowledge in pathology education. However, controlled
studies  are  needed  to  establish  causality.  In  addition,  the  pacing  challenges  identified  in  this  study  highlight  the  need  for  continued
curriculum refinement to balance the benefits of case-based learning against the cognitive demands it places on students. Future studies
should  also  adopt  multi-center  longitudinal  designs  with  control  groups  to  further  examine  the  long-term  learning  transfer  and
sustained educational effects of this teaching model.
Keywords: pathology education, curriculum reconstruction, case-driven learning, knowledge graph, clinical reasoning

Introduction
As a core bridge between basic and clinical medicine, pathology aims to build a coherent causal pathway linking disease
mechanisms to clinical manifestations.1 Traditional pathology teaching, organized around disciplinary divisions, has long
adopted  a  linear  structure  (eg,  presenting  etiology,  pathogenesis  and  pathological  changes  in  a  fixed  sequence).
Cognitively,  this  traditional  framework  has  several  limitations.  First,  course  content  is  structured  based  on  discipline--
based classification rather than clinical problem-solving logic, which tends to separate concepts that are closely related in

clinical  practice.  Second,  one-way  lecture-based  teaching  provides  few  opportunities  for  students  to  actively  develop
their  own  cognitive  frameworks  for  clinical  reasoning.  Together,  these  drawbacks  contribute  to  two  key  problems:
fragmented knowledge and inert knowledge. Consequently, students often struggle to apply theoretical knowledge to real
clinical scenarios.2

To  address  these  limitations,  this  study  introduces  a  case-centered  course  framework  for  pathology  education,
grounded  in  three  complementary  learning  theories.  First,  constructivism  provides  the  foundation  for  understanding
how  students  learn;  it  emphasizes  that  knowledge  is  actively  built  through  problem-solving,  rather  than  passively
memorized  as  isolated  facts.3  Second,  situated  learning  theory  suggests  that  separating  pathology  from  clinical  cases
contributes to inert knowledge.4  Although Case-Based Learning (CBL) was introduced to bridge this gap, conventional
CBL often tends to become a superficial process in which cases are treated as post-class supplements to textbook facts.5,6
Furthermore, the temporal and spatial separation between lectures and discussions may create a disconnect in learning,
requiring students to struggle with memory decay. Therefore, to shift the learning logic from textbook chapters to clinical
reasoning requires  elevating  clinical cases  to  the  organizing thread  of  knowledge.  Third,  because navigating these  real
cases requires intense thinking, cognitive load theory (CLT) provides the principles for managing this mental workload
and  minimizing  student  overload.7,8  Under  traditional  linear  teaching,  scattered  facts  have  high  element  interactivity,
creating  a  heavy  intrinsic  cognitive  burden  without  helping  students  build  structured  mental  frameworks.9  To  address
this,  knowledge  graph  (KG)  technology  can  be  introduced  as  a  powerful  visual  support  tool  that  explicitly  organizes
knowledge through semantic network modeling, thereby reducing cognitive overload and integrating fragmented content.
First,  by  explicitly  constructing  “entity-relation”  semantic  networks,  KG  transforms  fragmented,  isolated  knowledge
points  into  an  interconnected,  holistic  structure.  This  counters  the  problem  of  knowledge  fragmentation  by  visually
demonstrating the causal, hierarchical, and sequential relationships between concepts (eg, linking a specific pathological
change to its etiology and clinical manifestation). Second, from the perspective of cognitive load theory, the high element
interactivity  inherent  in  pathological  reasoning  is  a  major  source  of  intrinsic  cognitive  load.  A  KG  mitigates  this  by
providing  a  visual  schema  that  reorganizes  these  complex,  high-interactivity  elements  into  a  single,  low-interactivity
visual  representation.10  This  scaffolding  assists  students  in  instantly  integrating  fragmented  case  information  into
interconnected knowledge networks.11

To operationalize and validate these theoretical frameworks, this study developed a systematic, case-driven curricu-
lum restructuring model supported by a KG. The primary goal was to break away from conventional, textbook-aligned
linear instruction and reorient the curriculum around authentic clinical diagnostic reasoning. By embedding this model
into a standard undergraduate medical curriculum, the study may serve as a reference for educators seeking to address the
persistent challenge of inert knowledge and promoting higher-order clinical reasoning.

Materials and Methods
Construction Pathway of the Multimodal KG
This  study  constructed  a  multimodal  KG  for  the  pathology  curriculum  on  the  Wisdom  platform,  a  Chinese  online
education  platform  that  provides  built-in  tools  for  KG  construction  and  multimodal  resource  linking.  The  construction
process was operationalized as follows: (1) Construction of the foundational declarative KG (knowledge-oriented). First,
based strictly on the pathology syllabus, the research team extracted and established core knowledge elements to serve as
the  skeletal  nodes  of  the  graph.  The  curriculum  content  was  systematically  decomposed  into  105  knowledge  points.
Through  semantic  annotation  and  hierarchical  organization,  a  total  of  1490  knowledge  nodes  were  identified.  For
instance,  nodes such  as  “acute  inflammatory response”,  “granulation  tissue”,  and  “crescentic glomerulonephritis”  were
defined.  This  was  performed  manually  using  the  platform’s  node  creation  interface,  with  each  node  tagged  by  its
corresponding  chapter  and  topic.  Subsequently,  multimodal  instructional  resources,  including  68  micro-lecture  videos,
91  PPTs,  201  high-resolution  digital  slides,  and  690  exercises,  were  manually  linked  via  the  platform’s  resource
management  interface  to  their  corresponding  knowledge  nodes.  Finally,  using  the  platform’s  built-in  semantic  linking
function, a foundational knowledge network was established, wherein knowledge nodes functioned as entities and logical
semantics, such as pathological causality (“cause-of”) and clinical manifestation (“manifests-as”), constituted the edges.

level  was  built  upon  a  set  of  29  real  clinical  cases  or  clinical  application  scenarios  (eg,  “A  48-year-old  male  patient
developed  bilateral  ankle  pitting  edema  and  markedly  increased  foamy  urine  approximately  7  months  ago”).  Students
were  then  explicitly  guided  to  analyze  the  possible  pathological  changes,  differential  diagnosis  and  their  underlying
mechanisms based on the clinical presentation. This case-driven analysis was not conducted in isolation. Instead, it was
linked to the corresponding problems in the intermediate KG, and through that, access the specific knowledge nodes in
the foundational declarative KG. This three-tiered design (Knowledge-based network → problem-oriented →Applied-lev
el,  Supplementary  Figure  1)  allows  students  to  embed  the  pathological  mechanisms  of  a  specific  case  into  a  broader
conceptual framework.

Instructional Design and Implementation Pathway
Curriculum Restructuring Logic
This study replaces the traditional, linear, discipline-centered logic of pathology, which sequentially presents “concept,
etiology,  pathogenesis,  pathological  change,  and  clinical  manifestation”,  with  a  case-driven,  KG-supported  teaching
model.  Utilizing  authentic  clinical  cases  as  the  logical  entry  point,  this  model  guides  students  to  actively  extract  and
construct interconnected knowledge networks while engaging in clinical diagnostic-solving. To rigorously test the model
under  conditions  of  high  cognitive  load,  the  curriculum  intervention  was  implemented  within  the  “Urinary  System
Pathology” module. This module, particularly the section on glomerulonephritis, inherently imposes the highest intrinsic
cognitive  load  across  the  pathology  curriculum  due  to  its  highly  abstract  pathogenetic  mechanisms  and  sophisticated
morphological diagnostic criteria.

Participants and Instructors
The  participants  comprised  54  third-year  medical  students  from  an  intact  class  within  the  2024  five-year  clinical
medicine  program  at  Peking  University  Health  Science  Center.  To  control  for  implementation  bias,  all  instructional
sessions  were  delivered  by  the  same  senior  pathology  instructor,  who  possesses  over  10  years  of  experience  in  both
pathology  education  and  clinical  pathological  diagnosis  of  urinary  system  diseases.  Written  informed  consent  was
obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Implementation of the Restructured Curriculum
This  instructional  unit  comprised  a  total  of  6  class  hours,  delivered  across  three  progressive  sessions  (2  class  hours
per  session,  50  minutes  per  class  hour).  Each  session  followed  a  standardized  three-stage  process:  pre-session  autono-

overall  framework  of  the  reconstructed  pathology  curriculum.  To  demonstrate  how  this  model  covers  the  traditional

phenotypes.

③  Post-Session Reinforcement and Extension: Students accessed the intermediate (problem-oriented) and advanced

Session 2: Pyelonephritis and Renal Tumors (2 Class Hours)
The  structure  followed  that  of  Session  1,  utilizing  the  same  three-stage  process.  Four  cases  –pyelonephritis,  clear  cell
renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), nephroblastoma (Wilms’ tumor), and urothelial carcinoma, were employed for case-driven
instruction. Pre-session and post-session activities were identical to those described in Session 1.

Session 3: Advanced Cases and Comprehensive Case Analysis (2 Class Hours)
The three-stage structure was again followed. Utilizing three advanced cases-membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis
(MPGN),  IgA  nephropathy,  and  chronic  kidney  disease  (CKD)  complicated  by  renal  cell  carcinoma,  this  session
combined  instructor  guidance  with  small-group  discussions  to  perform  comprehensive  case  analysis  and  differential
diagnosis. Pre-session and post-session activities were identical to those described in Session 1.

Evaluation Methods and Implementation Procedures
To comprehensively and objectively evaluate the educational efficacy of this restructured model on students’ knowledge
retention,  this  study  established  a  mixed-methods  evaluation  framework  spanning  two  distinct  time  points:  immediate
and delayed assessments. This approach combined standardized testing (Supplementary Table 2) with a student experi-
ence  questionnaire.  The  specific  components  are  as  follows:  (1)  In-session  formative  testing  (Immediate  assessment):
Executed at the end of each session. Each quiz comprised 5 text-only multiple-choice questions (MCQs, specifically A1/
A2 types, 2 points per question). (2) Delayed retention testing: Administered 14 days after the conclusion of the entire
instructional unit, consisting of two components: ①  Open-book MCQ test: Included 25 A1/A2-type MCQs (2 points per
question).  During  the  test,  students  were  permitted  to  consult  the  multimodal  KG  to  assess  their  resource-assisted
information  retention  and  retrieval  proficiency.  ②  Closed-book  clinicopathological  analysis  test:  Conducted  following
the  open-book  test,  comprising  2  subjective  case-analysis  questions  (30  points  per  question).  This  assessment  strictly
enforced  a  closed-book  protocol,  designed  to  evaluate  whether  students could  autonomously  integrate  information  and
execute clinical reasoning after the complete fading of external cognitive scaffolds. All test items (both in-session testing
and delayed retention testing) were drawn from a centralized item bank developed by the same teaching team based on
the  unified  course  learning  objectives,  which  covers  all  core  knowledge  points  and  is  categorized  by  cognitive  level
according to Bloom’s taxonomy.12  The items in this bank have been validated through previous final examinations, with
difficulty indices ranging from 0.3 to 0.8 and discrimination indices exceeding 0.25. Each test was a new set of questions.
The  in-session  testing  targeted  the  core  content  of  each  respective  session,  while  the  delayed  open-book  MCQ  testing
comprehensively  covered  the  core  content  from  all  three  sessions.  To  ensure  comparability  across  assessments,  the
proportion of easy, medium, and difficult items was held constant across all tests. Difficulty was classified by the item
difficulty index (P) as follows: easy items (0.65 < P ≤ 0.80), medium items (0.45 < P ≤ 0.65), and difficult items (0.30 <
P ≤ 0.45). All tests maintained the same ratio of items across these three difficulty levels. In addition, the two subjective

Grading Process and Reliability Control for Subjective Questions
To guarantee the objectivity and stability of grading for the subjective clinicopathological analysis questions, this study
utilized a structured rubric and a standardized grading workflow. First, questions were scored using a structured rubric
including  three  scoring  units:  pathological  diagnosis  (20  points)  assessing  the  accuracy  of  the  diagnostic  term  and  the
supporting rationale, pathogenesis (20 points) evaluating the identification of core mechanisms and the integration of the
reasoning chain from etiology to pathology, and clinicopathological correlations (20 points) measuring the ability to link
pathological findings to clinical symptoms and treatment principles, as well as the accuracy of the applied knowledge.
Each dimension was further subdivided into specific scoring units with detailed criteria for different performance levels
(Supplementary Table 3). Second, two raters with both pathology teaching and extensive clinical pathological diagnostic
experience collaboratively reviewed the rubric and independently graded 3 to 5 randomly selected pilot answer sheets to
calibrate  the  grading  standards.  Subsequently,  both  raters  conducted  independent,  blind  grading  of  all  student  answer
sheets. Finally, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was employed to calculate the inter-rater reliability based on
the total scores awarded by the two raters, with an ICC value ≥0.75 established as the threshold for good reliability. If the
ICC fell below this threshold, the two raters discussed the points of divergence to reach a consensus through negotiation.
The mean score from both raters was adopted as the final grade.

Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. To standardize the scoring scales across different tests and
facilitate  meaningful  comparisons,  all  raw  test  scores  were  converted  into  percentage  scores.  Specifically,  raw  scores
were standardized as a percentage of the total points within each level. Given that the percentage scores did not meet the
assumption of normality across different assessment time points, non-parametric tests were uniformly adopted to enhance
the robustness of the statistical outcomes. The overall comparisons of percentage scores across multiple time points were
conducted  using  the  Friedman  test.  If  the  Friedman  test  yielded  significant  results  (P  <  0.05),  post  hoc  pairwise
comparisons  were  further  executed  utilizing  the  Wilcoxon  signed-rank  test.  To  control  the  inflated  risk  of  Type
I  errors  arising  from  multiple  comparisons,  the  significance  levels  were  adjusted  using  the  Bonferroni  correction.  For
the questionnaire analysis, a paired t-test was employed to analyze changes in students’ self-perceived capabilities.

To  provide  a  more  nuanced  understanding  of  the  depth  of  learning,  we  classified  the  test  items  into  two  cognitive
levels  based  on  the  revised  Bloom’s  taxonomy:  “remembering/understanding”  (basic  recall  and  comprehension  of
pathological  concepts)  and  “applying/analyzing”  (higher-order  cognitive  tasks  requiring  application  of  knowledge  to
clinical scenarios and analysis of pathophysiological mechanisms). This classification was performed by two independent
researchers and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Day 14 Retention Test: Short-Term Knowledge Retention and Sustained Application Ability
We conducted two follow-up assessments 14 days after course completion, an open-book MCQs test and a closed-book
clinicopathological analysis test, to evaluate short-term knowledge retention and application performance with or without
reference materials. Outcomes were compared with the aggregated performance of the three in-session tests. The results
demonstrated  a  statistically  significant  difference  in  students’  median  percentage  scores  across  the  three  assessment
conditions (Table 1; Friedman χ2  = 18.0, P < 0.001).

Post  hoc  pairwise  comparisons  revealed  that  the  median  percentage  score  under  the  Day  14  open-book  condition
(91.8%)  was  significantly higher than  that of  the  in-session immediate  tests (83.5%,  adjusted P  < 0.001).  Notably,  the
median  score  under  the  Day  14  closed-book  condition  (89.0%)  was  also  significantly  higher  than  the  in-session
performance (83.5%, adjusted P = 0.016). No statistically significant difference in median scores was detected between
the Day 14 open-book and closed-book conditions (adjusted P = 0.69).

To  further  elucidate  educational  efficacy  at  distinct  cognitive  levels,  test  items  were  categorized  by  cognitive  level
(“remembering/understanding”  and  “applying/analyzing”),  followed  by  a  subgroup  analysis  of  the  median  percentage
scores  for  each  level  across  the  assessment  time  points.  The  analysis  indicated  that  at  the  remembering/understanding
level,  the  median  score  under  the  Day  14  open-book  condition  (95.4%)  was  significantly  higher  than  that  of  the  in-
session immediate tests (86.7%, adjusted P < 0.001).

For higher-order cognitive tasks (applying and analyzing), a statistically significant difference was also observed in
the median percentage scores across the three conditions (Friedman χ2 = 10.1, P = 0.006). Post hoc pairwise comparisons
demonstrated that the median score under the Day 14 open-book condition (88.5%) was significantly higher than that of
the  in-session  immediate  tests  (81.7%,  adjusted  P  =  0.018).  Mirroring  this  trend,  the  median  score  under  the  Day  14
closed-book condition (89.0%) was also significantly superior to the in-session performance (81.7%, adjusted P = 0.028).
Crucially, no statistically significant difference in median percentage scores was detected between the Day 14 open-book
and closed-book conditions at this high-order cognitive level (adjusted P = 1.0).

Discussion
This  study  implemented  and  evaluated  a  case-driven,  KG-supported  curriculum  restructuring  model,  utilizing  urinary
system pathology as the contextual framework. In this model, knowledge points were rooted within the logic of clinical
diagnosis  and  treatment.  We  observed  that,  as  the  course  progressed,  students  not  only  achieved  improvements  in
immediate knowledge acquisition but also demonstrated enhanced short-term knowledge retention at Day 14 under both
open-book  and  closed-book  conditions.  This  teaching  model  was  well  received  by  the  students,  which  may  offer
a reproducible framework for case-driven and technology-enhanced medical education.

Effects of the Case-Driven and KG-Supported Model in Knowledge Acquisition
Prior work has  demonstrated the feasibility of  integrating  KGs  into medical education. Based  on our  literature review,
most  existing  KG  constructions  have  remained  primarily  at  the  declarative  knowledge  level,  focusing  on  structuring
course content and visualizing relationships among knowledge points.11,13,14  A small number of studies have attempted
to build problem and competency layers in addition to the declarative knowledge level.10,15 For instance, Wei et al10 have
explicitly constructed a hierarchical “knowledge-problem-competency” KG that extends beyond knowledge organization
to model problem-oriented reasoning and clinical competency development in pathology teaching. Our KG construction
adopts  a  similar  three-tiered  architecture.  This  layer  design  is  intended  to  enable  students  to  progressively  move  from
memorizing  fragmented  declarative  knowledge,  to  analyzing  mechanism-based  clinical  problems,  and  ultimately  to
engaging in integrated clinical reasoning within a coherent conceptual framework.

KGs  have  been  used  as  supplementary  tools  across  pre-class,  in-class,  and  post-class  phases,10,11,13,14  typically
integrated within existing chapter-based curricula to support preview, instruction, review, and resource recommendation.
Although  our  study  shares  a  similar  technical  basis  and  workflow,  it  differs  in  how  the  curriculum  is  organized.

According to situated learning theory, separating pathological mechanisms from clinical realities tends to produce inert
knowledge;  thus,  instructions  grounded  in  real  patient  scenarios  are  essential  for  cultivating  clinical  reasoning.2,16,17
However,  the manner in which  clinical cases are integrated into the curriculum varies substantially  across studies, and
these differences have important implications for the depth and nature of student learning. In prior studies in pathology
teaching, the implementation of case-based instruction has predominantly followed two patterns. First, cases are used as
supplementary  materials  or  testing  tools  after  theoretical  lectures.14  Second,  cases  serve  as  the  materials  of  in-class
discussion  or  activities,18  but  the  overall  curriculum  structure  remains  primarily  organized  around  textbook  chapters.
Cases are often embedded within this chapter-based framework as tools for stimulating interest, discussion, or reasoning,
rather  than  as  the  fundamental  organizing  principle  of  the  curriculum  itself.  In  contrast,  our  study  reorganizes  the
curriculum  around  clinical  cases  as  the  primary  organizing  framework,  rather  than  being  driven  primarily  by  textbook
chapters. The learning sequence begins with a clinical case presentation, guides students through reverse reasoning from
symptoms  to  pathological  changes  and  mechanisms.  During  this  process,  students  encounter  and  learn  specific  knowl-
edge points as they are needed to solve the clinical problem at hand. This represents a move toward a curriculum where
cases play a more central organizing role in pathology teaching.

The teaching model achieved favorable educational outcomes, as reflected in student performance across all assess-
ments. The statistically significant improvement from the first to the second formative test suggests that students rapidly
adapted  to  the  course  format.  Although  the  difference  between  the  first  and  third  tests  showed  only  borderline
significance,  this  finding  is  likely  attributable  to  the  curricular  design  of  the  third  session,  which  comprised  advanced
cases and comprehensive clinical scenarios requiring higher-order synthesis. In other words, the increased cognitive load
imposed by the third session may have offset the expected upward trend in scores. The delayed retention test on Day 14
post-course further provides crucial empirical evidence. Under the closed-book condition where the KG was completely
removed,  students’  median  percentage  score  at  the  analyzing  and  applying  level  (89.0%)  demonstrated  no  statistically
significant  difference  compared  to  the  open-book  condition  with  KG  support  (88.5%),  and  both  were  significantly
superior to the immediate in-session performance (81.7%). These results suggest that the observed learning gains were
sustained  over  time,  rather  than  transient  memorization.  Moreover,  students’  performance  may  indicate  a  progressive
shift from resource-dependent performance to more autonomous knowledge retrieval.

Higher-Order Capacity Gains and Its Accompanying Pacing Challenges
The  questionnaire  findings  reveal  a  discrepancy  between  students’  perceived  learning  gains  and  their  classroom
experience. On one hand, students widely agreed that the curriculum stimulated their interest and improved their clinical
reasoning skills. However, this positive feedback contrasted with their evaluation of the instructional pacing: only 40.4%
strongly agreed that the workload and pace were moderate, while 14.8% felt neutral or negative. To examine the sources
of these pacing difficulties, we consider two interrelated factors: (1) in-session cognitive demands, and (2) the density of
in-session activities. By anchoring learning within clinical contexts, this model organizes complex pathology knowledge
into  a  clinical  framework,  saving  students  from  struggling  with  disconnected  textbook  concepts.  However,  this  design
simultaneously introduces a new kind of cognitive demand. Because classroom activities focus on higher-order clinical
reasoning, students must constantly perform intense mental tasks, such as extracting key data, reorganizing knowledge,
and  making  clinical analysis.  This  sudden  shift  from  passive  listening  to  continuous  high-level  thinking  increases  task
complexity.  The  density  of  in-session  activities  further  compounds  these  demands.  Each  session  is  structured  around
multiple  case-based  problems  that  require  sustained  active  participation.  The  cumulative  effect  above  may  be  the  root
cause of why a subset of students felt the class pace was too fast and the workload was overwhelming.

This phenomenon is by no means unique to our model; it is a common challenge in educational reforms that target
high-order  thinking.  Studies  on  TBL,19  and  PBL/CBL20  have  consistently  reported  that  while  these  active  learning
methods  successfully  improve  higher-order  skills,  they  also  impose  increased  cognitive  load  on  students  due  to  the
intensive collaboration, reasoning, and self-directed information synthesis they require. The pacing issues observed in our
model  reflect  this  same  pattern  within  the  specific  context  of  pathology  education.  However,  it  is  noteworthy  that
students’  concerns  about  the  fast  pace  did  not  reduce  their  overall  satisfaction  with  the  course.  In  fact,  all  surveyed
students  still  felt  that  this  model  was  better  than  traditional  lecturing,  and  unanimously  agreed  that  it  stimulated  their

Instructional Optimization: Managing Cognitive Demands Across Teaching Phases
Based  on  CLT  and  our  survey  findings,  future  teaching  improvements  should  focus  on  managing  students’  mental
workload  through  the  following  steps.  To  address  the  density  of  in-session  activities  and  the  sustained  high-level
cognitive  engagement,  we  can  adopt  a  step-by-step  approach  that  combines  short  lecture  segments  with  immediate
practice. The teaching process for each clinical case can be divided into several small parts, each lasting 10 to 15 minutes.
After completing each part, such as discussing a clinical diagnosis or a pathological mechanism, a short quiz or activity
(3  to  5  minutes)  will  be  introduced.  This  could  involve  using  one  or  two  MCQs  to  check  understanding,  or  asking
students to explain the key ideas to their peers. This alternating lecture-and-practice design serves two main purposes: (1)
it breaks down complex cases into smaller, manageable pieces, lowering the information density and making the session
feel  less  rushed;  (2)  these  quick  exercises  leverage  the  testing  effect  to  consolidate  learning  and  reduce  cognitive
overload.22

Second,  to  address  the  varying  levels  of  student  preparedness  and  ability,  which  may  influence  how  individual
students experience pre-session preparation and in-session cognitive demands, we can use KGs to provide personalized
learning paths and different levels of support based on each student’s current ability. For high-performing students, the
system  will  provide  access  to  challenging  cases  from  the  advanced  KG.  In  contrast,  for  students  who  struggle,  it  will
prioritize  simpler  cases.  This  setup  ensures  that  the  task  difficulty  matches  each  student’s  actual  level,  preventing
cognitive overload.23

Limitations and Future Directions
There  were  several  limitations  in  this  study.  First,  this  study  adopted  a  single-group,  longitudinal  design  and  lacked
a fully parallel, blinded control group undergoing traditional instruction, which inherently weakens the strength of causal
inference regarding the model’s independent effect. Second, the study sample was derived from a single natural class (n =
54) at a single medical institution. The relatively small sample size necessitates further multi-institutional validation to
confirm the generalizability of this model. Finally, the delayed retention test on Day 14 post-course only captures short-
term knowledge retention. Whether this model exerts a sustained positive impact on medical students’ long-term transfer
of learning, specifically, their long-term clinical reasoning performance during subsequent clinical clerkships and hospital
rotations, remains to be verified by longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods.

Conclusion
The long-standing dilemma in pathology education stems largely from a mismatch between how knowledge is organized
by  discipline  and  how  students  naturally  process  information  in  clinical  contexts.  This  study  examined  the  value  of
a “case-driven and KG-supported” teaching model that changes clinical cases from supplementary exercises into the core
framework  that  guides  the  entire  learning  process,  thereby  shifting  the  teaching  focus  from  “discipline-centric”  to
“clinical-reasoning” logic.

We observed that this new approach was associated with improved high-order cognitive performance among medical
students in our cohort. Notably, students were able to independently apply their clinical reasoning skills even after the
KGs and instructor guidance were completely removed. However, a controlled study is required to determine whether the
intervention  itself  caused  the  observed  improvement.  In  addition,  further  longitudinal  research  is  needed  to  manage
cognitive demands across different learning phases and to assess long-term retention.
